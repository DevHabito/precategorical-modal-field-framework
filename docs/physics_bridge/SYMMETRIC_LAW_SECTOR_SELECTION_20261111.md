# Symmetric law, asymmetric realization — sector-selection audit

**Date:** 2026-11-11  
**Author:** Felipe Gianini Romero  
**Status:** exact finite stochastic sector-selection witness + symmetry-breaking boundary  
**Novelty status:** the distinction between symmetric laws/measures and asymmetric sectors is classical; the finite witness is project-specific bookkeeping, not a new SSB theorem

## 1. Why this audit is necessary

The symmetry-orientation no-go established that a deterministic, symmetry-equivariant rule cannot canonically select one strict orientation from a pair exchanged by a symmetry.

That statement does not imply that every individual realization of a symmetric stochastic theory must itself remain symmetric.

A symmetric probability law can assign equal weight to two asymmetric sectors.

This distinction is important for the polarity problem.

## 2. Minimal exact model

Take three states

$$
-,\quad 0,\quad +.
$$

Let reflection \(\mathcal R\) exchange

$$
+\leftrightarrow-
$$

and fix \(0\).

Use the stochastic matrix, in the ordered basis \((-,0,+)\),

$$
K=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0\\
1/2&0&1/2\\
0&0&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Thus:

- \(+\) is absorbing;
- \(-\) is absorbing;
- the neutral state \(0\) selects either sector with equal probability.

The initial distribution is

$$
\pi_0=(0,1,0).
$$

It is exactly reflection invariant.

## 3. Symmetry of the law

The kernel commutes with reflection.

Equivalently,

$$
K(\mathcal R x,\mathcal R A)
=
K(x,A).
$$

Neither \(+\) nor \(-\) is preferred by the stochastic law.

After one update,

$$
\boxed{
\pi_1=
\left(
\frac12,0,\frac12
\right).
}
$$

Because both outer states are absorbing,

$$
\pi_n=\pi_1
$$

for all \(n\ge1\).

The ensemble remains exactly symmetric.

## 4. Order parameter

Define

$$
M(-)=-1,
\qquad
M(0)=0,
\qquad
M(+)=1.
$$

At the ensemble level,

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb E_{\pi_n}[M]=0
}
$$

for every \(n\).

But for every realization after sector selection,

$$
M\in\{-1,+1\}
$$

with probability one.

Therefore for \(n\ge1\),

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb E_{\pi_n}[|M|]=1
}
$$

while

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb E_{\pi_n}[M]=0.
}
$$

The law has no preferred polarity.

Individual realizations do.

## 5. What has and has not emerged

### Realization-level polarity

After selection, a sample lies in either the \(+\) or \(-\) sector.

This polarity was not biased toward one sign in the law.

### Theory-level canonical polarity

None exists.

The probability of each sign is exactly one half.

If the full theory is reflected,

$$
+\leftrightarrow-,
$$

its predictions are unchanged.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{sector selection}
\neq
\text{canonical polarity}.
}
$$

## 6. Refinement of the symmetry no-go

The deterministic no-go should therefore be read as:

> A symmetric primitive structure cannot deterministically and equivariantly select one uniquely preferred reversal-odd orientation.

It should **not** be read as:

> Every realization generated by a symmetric law must itself be symmetric.

The stochastic case instead gives:

$$
\boxed{
\text{symmetric law}
\Longrightarrow
\text{symmetric distribution over reversed sectors},
}
$$

while allowing

$$
\boxed{
\text{individual sample}
\Longrightarrow
\text{asymmetric sector}.
}
$$

## 7. Relation to A21

A21 couples two symmetry-related latent models with the same model-independent random tape and proves that an equivariant endogenous law cannot distinguish latent representatives that produce the same relational history.

The present witness asks a different question.

It does not try to identify one hidden representative.

It asks whether one symmetric law can produce a realization with a nonzero order parameter.

There is no contradiction.

At the ensemble level the symmetry remains exact.

## 8. Static formulation

The essential distinction does not require the transition model.

One may define a symmetric probability measure directly on two sectors:

$$
\mu
=
\frac12\delta_+
+
\frac12\delta_-.
$$

Reflection exchanges the two pure sectors and leaves \(\mu\) invariant.

An observed realization can belong to one pure sector while the probability measure remains symmetric.

This static version is especially relevant to a timeless pregeometric discussion.

It shows that **sector asymmetry and ensemble symmetry can coexist without defining temporal succession**.

## 9. Double-well benchmark

The classical symmetric double-well potential

$$
V(m)=(m^2-1)^2
$$

has two degenerate minima,

$$
m=+1,
\qquad
m=-1,
$$

exchanged by the \(\mathbb Z_2\) symmetry

$$
m\mapsto-m.
$$

The potential does not prefer one minimum.

Each minimum is individually asymmetric.

This is the elementary static geometry behind symmetry-broken sectors.

In finite quantum systems the status of spontaneous symmetry breaking is subtler: exact finite ground states often retain the symmetry, and the thermodynamic/classical limit, perturbations, preparation, or exceptional finite-size structures become important.

Therefore the present finite stochastic witness should **not** be called a proof of physical spontaneous symmetry breaking.

## 10. Thermodynamic-limit boundary

In standard statistical and many-body theory, spontaneous symmetry breaking is associated with multiple symmetry-related pure phases/sectors and often with an infinite-volume or thermodynamic-limit construction.

A symmetric Gibbs state may be decomposable into symmetry-related extremal phases.

Finite systems can display long-lived symmetry-broken behavior or special finite-size effects, but the precise usage of “SSB” depends on the model and limiting procedure.

The repository therefore uses the neutral term:

> **stochastic sector selection**

for the finite witness above.

## 11. Recent finite-size caution

Recent work has shown that under specific parity and correlation conditions some finite-size quantum spin systems can display robust symmetry-broken behavior after a suitable quasiadiabatic preparation.

This is an important qualification to broad slogans such as “SSB is impossible in every finite system.”

It does not change the present logical point:

- symmetric laws can support symmetry-related asymmetric sectors;
- the mechanism and stability of sector selection are model dependent;
- a selected sector is not automatically a temporal order.

## 12. Consequence for the polarity problem

The previous synthesis asked whether polarity must be primitive or selected.

This audit provides a third logical possibility:

$$
\boxed{
\text{the law can remain symmetric while the realized sector is asymmetric}.
}
$$

Therefore polarity need not always be encoded as a bias in the fundamental law.

It may live in the realized state/sector.

However the theory still does not canonically predict

$$
+
$$

rather than

$$
-.
$$

It predicts a symmetric distribution over them.

## 13. Closed-universe interpretation

For a closed-universe pregeometric theory, sector selection would move the question from

> Why is the law asymmetric?

to

> Why is the actual universe in this asymmetric sector rather than its symmetry-related partner?

Possible answers could involve:

- contingent initial/boundary state;
- superselection;
- spontaneous symmetry breaking in an appropriate limit;
- quantum branching/decoherence;
- a measure over sectors;
- a deeper asymmetric structure.

None is established by the current repository.

## 14. Why random selection does not solve temporal order

Even if a sector supplies a signed polarity variable

$$
P=\pm1,
$$

that sign need not define:

- which event causally precedes another;
- a partial order;
- a clock;
- duration;
- a Lorentzian causal structure.

A ferromagnet choosing one magnetization direction is the obvious analogy.

The selected sign is physical but not temporal.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{realization-level polarity}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{time}.
}
$$

## 15. Exact finite regression

A Fraction-based exact audit verifies for steps \(n=0,\ldots,5\):

- reflection invariance of the distribution;
- \(\mathbb E[M]=0\);
- \(\mathbb E[|M|]=0\) at \(n=0\);
- \(\mathbb E[|M|]=1\) for every \(n\ge1\);
- exact equal sector probabilities \(1/2,1/2\).

No floating-point tolerance is used.

## 16. Current verdict

**Can a symmetric law produce asymmetric individual realizations?**  
YES.

**Can it prefer one polarity at the ensemble level without asymmetric information?**  
NO in this symmetric witness.

**Does this contradict the deterministic symmetry no-go?**  
NO.

**Is the finite witness itself physical SSB?**  
NO; it is a sector-selection toy model.

YES.

NO.

## 17. Revised polarity taxonomy

The project should now distinguish:

1. **law-level polarity** — asymmetric dynamics/laws;
2. **boundary-state polarity** — asymmetric preparation;
3. **operational polarity** — asymmetric input/output or intervention/response roles;
4. **sector-selected polarity** — symmetric law/ensemble, asymmetric realized sector;
5. **loop/chiral polarity** — signed gauge-invariant cycle information.

All five can be physically meaningful.

None is automatically equivalent to the origin of time.
