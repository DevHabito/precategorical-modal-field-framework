# Law-level polarity does not derive temporal order

**Date:** 2026-11-11  
**Author:** Felipe Gianini Romero  
**Status:** empirical physics benchmark for polarity in dynamical laws  
**Novelty status:** direct T violation in neutral mesons is established experimental physics

## 1. Why this benchmark matters

The direction programme now separates:

- static symmetry breaking;
- orientability;
- physical polarity;
- temporal order;
- duration.

Boundary-state asymmetry showed one way polarity can enter an already dynamical description.

A second possibility is that the physical law itself is not invariant under time reversal.

This is not merely hypothetical.

Direct T violation has been observed experimentally.

## 2. BABAR benchmark

The BABAR Collaboration compared pairs of T-conjugated transitions in entangled neutral B mesons.

A representative comparison is of the form

$$
\bar B^0\to B_-
$$

versus

$$
B_-\to\bar B^0.
$$

The collaboration reported nonzero T-violating parameters, including approximately

$$
\Delta S_T^+
=
-1.37
\pm0.14_{\rm stat}
\pm0.06_{\rm syst},
$$

and

$$
\Delta S_T^-
=
1.17
\pm0.18_{\rm stat}
\pm0.11_{\rm syst}.
$$

The result was reported as a direct observation of time-reversal violation through exchange of initial and final states.

This is established particle physics, not a project result.

## 3. What T violation supplies

Within the established quantum theory, T violation means that a physical transition and its time-reversed counterpart need not have identical probabilities.

Thus the law contains a measurable reversal-odd asymmetry.

This is a genuine example of **law-level polarity**.

It differs logically from:

- choosing an asymmetric initial state under a symmetric law;
- adding a static distinguishing mark;
- selecting a magnetic loop chirality;
- coarse-graining memory.

## 4. Why this does not derive time

The symmetry operation \(T\) is defined as time reversal inside an already formulated temporal quantum theory.

The experimental comparison refers to time evolution and to exchanged initial/final states.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{T violation distinguishes temporal directions inside a temporal theory}.
}
$$

It does not establish:

$$
\boxed{
\text{the existence of temporal order from a timeless substrate}.
}
$$

Using observed T violation to solve the pregeometric origin-of-time problem directly would therefore be circular.

## 5. T violation and the thermodynamic arrow are different questions

A microscopic T-asymmetric interaction does not automatically explain why macroscopic thermodynamic systems exhibit the familiar entropy arrow.

The grounding of thermodynamic asymmetry remains a separate problem involving:

- microscopic dynamics;
- statistical mechanics;
- boundary conditions;
- coarse graining;
- cosmological structure;
- or other model-specific ingredients.

Current foundational literature continues to treat the thermodynamic arrow as a separate explanandum even when fundamental dynamics may contain T-asymmetric sectors.

Therefore the repository must not infer

$$
\text{weak-interaction T violation}
\Rightarrow
\text{thermodynamic arrow explained}.
$$

## 6. Polarity-source taxonomy

The current physical audits now contain three distinct sources of polarity.

### Law-level polarity

The dynamical law assigns different weights/probabilities to T-conjugated processes.

Example: neutral-B T violation.

### Boundary/state polarity

The law respects a symmetry but the preparation does not.

Example: mirrored initial occupancies under the same reflection-symmetric Markov kernel; Past-Hypothesis-type logic.

### Operational polarity

The primitive roles are asymmetric.

Example: intervention versus response in a causal experiment.

These mechanisms are not interchangeable.

## 7. A fourth possibility: sector selection

A symmetric law and symmetric space of possibilities can admit asymmetric sectors or solutions.

A realized sector can then carry polarity.

This includes spontaneous-symmetry-breaking-style logic.

But the selected sector is additional state information.

A theory must still explain:

- the space of alternatives;
- the selection mechanism or measure;
- stability of the selected sector;
- what observable detects it.

This route must not hide the selection in labels.

## 8. What each mechanism presupposes

| Source | Where asymmetry lives | Does it derive time? |
|---|---|---|
| T-asymmetric law | dynamics/law | No; T is already defined |
| special boundary state | preparation/state | No; evolution parameter already exists |
| intervention-response structure | operational roles | No; causal operation semantics are supplied |
| asymmetric sector | realized state/solution | No by itself; sector-selection semantics needed |
| loop holonomy/affinity | gauge-invariant cycle class | No; chirality/drive is not precedence |

Thus a physical source of polarity is still not equivalent to a source of temporal ontology.

## 9. Relation to the symmetry no-go

The symmetry theorem says that a perfectly symmetric primitive structure cannot canonically choose a reversal-odd output.

The polarity taxonomy tells us how established physics avoids that obstruction:

- the law can be asymmetric;
- the boundary state can be asymmetric;
- the operational type structure can be asymmetric;
- one asymmetric sector can be selected.

In every case there is identifiable asymmetric information somewhere.

The no-go is not violated.

## 10. Consequence for pregeometry

A future timeless theory must decide where its polarity lives.

If it is in a primitive law, that law needs non-temporal semantics.

If it is in a boundary condition, the theory still needs an independent notion of order relative to which “boundary” is meaningful.

If it is in an operational input/output structure, that structure must be reconstructed internally for a closed universe.

If it is in sector selection, the selection rule must be specified.

The question cannot be answered by writing an arrow on \(R\).
